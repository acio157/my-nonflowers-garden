# Pollock — style reference

The fact base a Pollock constraint is built against. Jackson Pollock (American, born Cody, Wyoming 1912, died East Hampton, New York 1956). We build to his signature **drip / pour period, roughly 1947 to 1950** (Full Fathom Five 1947 through One: Number 31 and Autumn Rhythm 1950). His earlier work (Composition with Pouring II, 1943; the Surrealist She-Wolf) still used brushes and tube paint; his late work turned darker and partly figurative again. The drip years are the grammar.

Tags: FACT (documented by the institution or a conservation study read directly), OBSERVED (visually true of the work but not stated in those words by the pages read), INTERPRETIVE (critical or biographical reading that varies by author).

## Technique
- FACT: canvas laid unstretched on the floor; Pollock "dropped, dribbled, or threw paint" and "flung and poured ropes of paint across the surface," working from all four sides. "I feel nearer, more a part of the painting... I can walk around it, work from the four sides and literally be in the painting" (Pollock, via MoMA on One: Number 31).
- FACT: tools were not conventional. He applied paint "sometimes straight from the can or with sticks and stiffened brushes," and also "by punching holes in the bottom of paint cans, squeezing it directly from tubes, and even using a turkey baster" (MoMA).
- FACT: the marks are lines and threads, not shapes. "Strings and skeins of enamel... weave and run, an intricate web," "looping cords of color," registering "force and speed" (MoMA).
- FACT: he insisted the chaos was controlled. He "worked spontaneously with admitted chance effects" but "asserted that he maintained control" (Hirshhorn). "Technique is just a means of arriving at a statement" (Pollock, via MoMA).
- INTERPRETIVE: the origin is Surrealist automatism, the "embrace of accident as a way to bypass the conscious mind," extended to "the chance effects of gravity and momentum on falling paint" (MoMA). Also cited: Navajo sand painting, Hans Hofmann, and 1930s muralism (Benton, Siqueiros).

## Materials
- FACT: enamel / house paint, not artists' tube paint, in the mature drips. The Met catalogues Autumn Rhythm and others as "Enamel on canvas." A Smithsonian/Hirshhorn conservation study of Number 3, 1949: Tiger found the paints were "with few exceptions... oil-modified alkyd paints," commercial architectural coatings, a shift from the artists' oil paints he used in 1943.
- FACT: he wanted flow. Pollock said only that he preferred a "liquid, flowing kind of paint" (Hirshhorn).
- FACT: matte and glossy coexist in one work. "Some of the paints are matte, while others are glossy," and "strings and skeins of enamel, some matte, some glossy" (Hirshhorn; MoMA).
- FACT: line weight varies within a work, "thick to thin and drawn out" (Hirshhorn).
- FACT (a specific outlier): Full Fathom Five embeds real objects, "nails, tacks, buttons, key, coins, cigarettes, matches," under dripped black and silver enamel (MoMA). Not typical, but documents how physical the surface is.

## Palette
Work-dependent, not a fixed set. What recurs across the drip paintings: black and white as structural, earth tones (tan, brown), a cool gray or blue or teal, on a light ground, with brighter accents in some works.
- FACT: One: Number 31, 1950 (MoMA): "an intricate web of tans, blues, and grays lashed through with black and white."
- FACT: Autumn Rhythm: Number 30, 1950 (Met; corroborated by secondary sources): black, white, and brown with a small amount of teal / grayish blue, over a light beige ground; he began with "a skein of thin black lines," then added browns and white with a little teal.
- FACT: Number 3, 1949: Tiger (Hirshhorn): "orange, silver, yellow, green, white, and black."
- FACT: Full Fathom Five (MoMA): dripped "black and silver enamels" over a built-up underlayer.
- FACT (earlier, pre-drip, for contrast): Composition with Pouring II, 1943 (Hirshhorn): brushed tube paints in yellow, blue, red, green, gray, with a final glossy black house paint.
- OBSERVED: the ground is usually a warm light neutral (raw canvas, tan, beige) that the poured colors sit on. Stated for Autumn Rhythm, visually true of others, not put in those words everywhere.

## Composition and surface
- FACT: "allover." The marks extend "across the entire surface of the canvas," edge to edge (MoMA, on Full Fathom Five as an early allover work).
- FACT: no single focal point. Works "have neither a single point of focus nor any obvious repetition or pattern," yet "sustain a sense of underlying order" (MoMA).
- FACT: threads are offset by pools. "A field of densely interlaced threads of paint offset by pools and splashes of color" (MoMA on One: Number 31).
- FACT: wet-in-wet and layered both occur. Colors "blend and bleed into each other" where applied close in time; elsewhere a lower layer dried before the next went on (Hirshhorn). The order of colors is sometimes impossible to reconstruct; he went "back and forth between colors."
- INTERPRETIVE: read as choreography or dance (the trace of his movement), as "the nervous intensity of the modern city," or as "the primal rhythms of nature"; the title Autumn Rhythm suggests "alignment with nature's constant flux" (MoMA; Met).

## Practical translation notes (our distillation, not new claims)
For the flower generator, Elizabeth's brief is: keep the flower silhouette, but multicolor the petals and make the edges read as splattered rather than jagged.
- Kill the jaggedness first. The engine's ragged petal edge comes from noise on the outline. Pollock is not a rough edge, it is flung droplets and threads landing on and around a form. Smooth the base petal, then let a splatter pass do the breaking up. Jag plus splatter reads as mud.
- Splatter, not grid. Unlike Kusama's regular dotted field, Pollock is random: scattered droplets at random positions, mixed radii (many fine flecks plus a few fat blobs), and a few thrown threads (thin curved lines). This is the parked scratch block below, turned from a lattice into chaos.
- Multicolor within one flower. Not one hue and not a smooth two-hue gradient. Several distinct colors coexist, applied in bursts. Anchor colors: black and white (structural), plus earth (tan, brown), plus one cool (gray-blue or teal); brighter accents (orange, yellow, green, silver-gray) optional and work-dependent.
- Matte plus glossy, thick plus thin. Vary droplet size and line weight so the field is not uniform.
- Allover, no focus. Spread the splatter across the whole bloom rather than clustering it at a center or arraying it to the petal, unlike Kusama's petal-following dots.
- This is the inverse of Miró: many marks not few, chance not deliberate placement, dense allover not floating signs on emptiness.

# Parked scratch — the splatter mechanism

Not wired in. This was the dot block cut from `herbal()`; kept as the seed for the Pollock splatter pass. For Pollock the grid loop gets replaced by randomized positions and radii (and optionally a few thrown threads), and the containment (`source-atop` clip vs flinging past the petal edge) is an open decision.

```js
if (PAR.dots){
  var db = Layer.bound(lay1)
  lay1.globalCompositeOperation = "source-atop"
  var step = 26
  var maxr = step*0.36
  for (var gx = db.xmin; gx < db.xmax; gx += step){
    for (var gy = db.ymin; gy < db.ymax; gy += step){
      var wave = 0.5 + 0.5*sin((gx*0.7 + gy)*0.02)
      var dr = maxr * (0.25 + 0.75*wave)
      dot(lay1, gx, gy, dr, PAR.dotColor)
    }
  }
  lay1.globalCompositeOperation = "source-over"
}
```

Reusable part: the bounded, clipped overlay. Replace-for-Pollock part: the grid loop, swapped for random splatter.
