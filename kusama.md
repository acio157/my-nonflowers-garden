# Kusama — style reference

The fact base every Kusama constraint is built against. Yayoi Kusama (born 1929, Matsumoto, Japan). The reference "grammar" we build to is her dot / net / flower vocabulary: the Infinity Nets (late 1950s to 60s), the Accumulation dot works (early 1960s), the pumpkins (central from the 1980s), and the flower sculptures and paintings (Flowers That Bloom at Midnight, since 2009; I Spend Each Day Embracing Flowers, 2023).

Tags: FACT (documented by the institution or literally described in a source read), OBSERVED (visually true of her work but NOT stated in words by the authoritative pages read, so not institution-sourced), INTERPRETIVE (symbolic or biographical, attributed to Kusama or her curators).

## Polka dots
- FACT: the polka dot is her single most identified motif (Britannica: "known for her extensive use of polka dots").
- FACT: the organizing principle is accumulation, one mark repeated by the thousands until it covers the whole field, edge to edge, with no single focal point (The Broad). Her titles use the word: Accumulation (1952), Accumulation of Nets No. 7 (1962).
- FACT: dots cover every surface and dissolve the object into the field. In the mirror rooms "the dots surround and engulf you, making it hard to tell where you end and where the room begins" (Tate). The Obliteration Room has visitors cover a white room in dots.
- INTERPRETIVE (her words): the dot is cosmic and defiant. "Our earth is like one little polka dot." "I would stand up to them all with a single polka dot." (Kusama, via Tate.)
- OBSERVED (not institution-sourced): high contrast between dot and ground, and graded/varied dot size within a field. Visually true, but the pages read did not state it in those words. Use it, but know it is our observation.

## Infinity nets
- FACT: near-monochrome paintings built from a single repeated looping arc-like brushstroke laid over the whole canvas, "accumulating by the thousands, giving the field a dense web of touches" (The Broad, on a ~33 ft 1961 painting).
- FACT: the net and the dot are the same accumulation read two ways. The painted mesh of arcs and its negative cells are inverse readings of one field (The Broad describes the mesh; the "net over ground / ground over net" phrasing is our summary).
- FACT: the term is long-running. MoMA catalogues Infinity Nets as early as 1951; her 2017 David Zwirner show was titled Infinity Nets.
- INTERPRETIVE (her account): the net came from a hallucination that "spilled over" the canvas onto the desk, floor, and window until "the entire room was covered with red net" (Kusama to Index, 1998, via The Broad).

## Color
- FACT: two distinct registers.
  - Austere near-monochrome for the Infinity Nets (Tate ties her "prim, monochrome suits" to her "stark, monochrome Infinity Net paintings").
  - High-key, saturated, multicolor for the dots, pumpkins, and flowers. QAGOMA: "polka-dotted planes of vivid colour," a "turn toward brighter, bolder hues." Zwirner: the flowers are "brightly colored."
- FACT: strong two-tone contrast pairings are documented in specific works, red-and-white (Infinity Mirror Room, Phalli's Field, 1965), red urethane pumpkin (2015), the classic yellow-and-black pumpkin.
- OBSERVED / CAUTION: "black grounds" as a named device. Her black-ground works exist, but no page read states it in words. Treat as observation.

## Form
- FACT: a rounded, bulbous, organic sculptural vocabulary. Zwirner: "fantastically scaled plants" with "exaggerated features"; pumpkins are "massive undulating" forms.
- FACT (pumpkins): squat, ribbed, bulbous gourds covered in a graduated dot grid; central to her work from the 1980s; the open-air Pumpkin (1994) on Naoshima. Kusama: pumpkins "speak to me of the joy of living."
- FACT (flowers, from QAGOMA's curator): "shiny surface, polka-dotted petals and great, staring eye," "sleek flowing lines and vibrant colour combinations," "floral forms that are at once simplified and fantastical." So the verifiable flower grammar is: rounded, full, simplified petals; thick curving stems; a prominent central eye/pistil; dot-covered surfaces; high-gloss finish; jarring bright color.
- INTERPRETIVE (attributed): the flowers embody a duality "between life and death, celebration and mourning... figuration and abstraction" (Zwirner; QAGOMA).

## Documented origin and meaning
Attributed to Kusama's own account and presented by institutions as her experience, not anonymous fact.
- The tablecloth / red-flowers hallucination (Kusama, via Tate): "after gazing at a pattern of red flowers on the tablecloth I looked up to see... the entire room, my entire body, and the entire universe covered with red flowers." Tate: "She felt as if she was disappearing... She describes this as 'self-obliteration.'"
- Dots as obliteration (Tate): "In Kusama's visual hallucinations, she becomes 'obliterated' by repeated dots."
- Themes the institutions foreground: infinity, repetition, obsession, accumulation, self-obliteration.
- Biography (documented): prone to hallucinations from an early age due to mental illness (The Broad); has lived and worked in a Tokyo psychiatric facility by her own choice since 1977 (Tate, Britannica).
- CAUTION: the specific "talking violets, each with a human face" wording is attributed to Kusama and consistent with QAGOMA's "communicating ominously" and her novel Violet Obsession, but the exact phrasing came from a search snippet, not a primary page read.

## Practical translation notes (our distillation, not new claims)
- Core operation is accumulation: one primitive (dot, or net cell) repeated to a dense all-over field with no privileged focal point.
- Two color regimes: austere near-monochrome nets, or saturated high-key multicolor with strong two-tone contrast (yellow/black, red/white, red/black documented).
- Figure and ground are reversible: the mesh and its negative cells are one accumulation read two ways.
- Forms are rounded, bulbous, simplified but fantastical; flowers = full petals, thick curving stems, central eye/pistil; dot-covered.
- Scale extremes: a tiny repeated unit against an environment-filling whole.
- This is the inverse of Miró: fill not air, many not few, multicolor not one primary, pattern-covered not flat.
